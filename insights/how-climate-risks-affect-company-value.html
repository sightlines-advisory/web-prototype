EXPLAINER · AASB S2: RISK MANAGEMENT

How do climate risks affect company value?

Follow physical and transition risks from business operations to financial consequences.

Susan McHattie | Sightlines AdvisoryNovember 20266 min read (estimated)First disclosure

Share on LinkedIn

Explore this subject further: How are climate scenarios useful?; Five steps to a confident start on climate reporting.

A manufacturer depends on reliable water supplies, affordable electricity and customers who need its products. A change in any of those conditions can affect what it earns, what it spends and where it invests.

Climate analysis helps management investigate those changes. AASB S2 uses two broad risk categories:

  • Physical risks arise from changing climate conditions and events.
  • Transition risks arise as policy, technology, markets and expectations change in the move towards a lower-carbon economy.

Both can affect operations, suppliers and customers, with consequences for financial performance, assets and cash flow.

Follow the risk through the business. Establish what changes, how it affects value and which decisions management can influence.

Physical risks affect the conditions in which you operate

Physical risks are commonly classified as

  • acute risks associated with events such as storms and floods, or
  • chronic risks associated with longer-term shifts such as rising average temperatures and sea levels. The effect on a company depends on its exposure, vulnerability and capacity to respond.

The following hypothetical examples illustrate relationships to investigate in your own business; they do not predict the financial consequences for any particular company.

An insured loss may cover part of the damage while leaving the company exposed to deductibles, delays, customer losses or higher renewal costs. Assess the costs and recovery assumptions that apply to the particular business.

Transition risks affect the economics of doing business

The established Taskforce for Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD) categories are policy and legal, technology, market and reputation. They help leaders identify different sources of change and connect them to financial consequences.

Category and risk examplePossible business impactPossible financial consequencesResponse and related opportunity
Policy and legal - tighter emissions requirements or carbon costsHigher compliance costs or a need to change production. Unsupported environmental claims may also expose the company to disputes or enforcement.Higher operating expenses, legal costs and capital requirements.Compare production options and substantiate claims. Lower-emissions capability may help meet purchasing requirements or improve cost competitiveness.
Technology - a lower-emissions substitute becomes commercially competitiveExisting equipment or products lose competitiveness before their planned retirement.Reduced margins, stranded development expenditure, shorter asset lives or impairment.Test upgrade, partnership and replacement options. New technology may support a better product, lower operating costs or a new service model.
Market - customers change specifications or suppliers change pricesDemand shifts between products, while energy and material costs change.Revenue changes, margin pressure and additional working-capital needs.Validate customer demand and adapt the offer. Resource efficiency, alternative materials or circular business models may improve the commercial proposition.
Reputation - stakeholders challenge a company’s claims or conductLoss of trust affects purchasing, recruitment or relationships.Lost sales, additional recruitment costs and expenditure to address concerns.Align statements with evidence and delivery. Demonstrable product performance and credible communication may strengthen customer relationships.

These are possible pathways. A risk category does not establish a financial impact. A customer’s stated preference needs to be tested against purchasing behaviour before you assume it will change revenue.

Use the analysis within enterprise risk management

Frameworks such as ISO 31000 and COSO ERM are established processes for assessing uncertainty, setting responsibilities and monitoring responses. Climate analysis can inform strategic, operational, financial and compliance risks already managed through those processes and introduce new risks or increase likelihood and impact assessments.

AASB S2 requires reporting entities to disclose how climate risk processes are integrated into overall risk management. APRA’s CPG 229 addresses integration for APRA-regulated institutions, while also identifying liability risk explicitly.

In the register, describe the cause, event and consequence clearly. For example:

Because our distribution centre and its main access road are exposed to flooding, a flood could interrupt deliveries, causing lost sales, stock damage and additional fulfilment costs.

That statement gives Operations, Finance and Risk a shared starting point.

Register fieldWhat to capture
Risk and affected objectiveDescribe the cause, event and consequence. Identify the value driver, asset, operation or customer commitment affected.
Classification and connectionsRecord the physical or transition category and link it to existing enterprise risks, including supply-chain and infrastructure dependencies.
Evidence and assumptionsIdentify sources, past events, observed effects and gaps. Separate company experience from projections and estimates.

Check for overlap when aggregating impacts. The same disrupted sale may appear in a site, supply-chain and customer risk assessment.

Connect risk responses with value creation

The Taskforce for Climate-related Financial Disclosures opportunity categories cover resource efficiency, energy sources, products and services, markets and resilience. These provide prompts for exploring how a company could respond.

The Climate Value Framework© starts with how the enterprise creates value, investigates relevant risks and opportunities, tests company evidence and assesses future financial consequences. Management can then compare responses by their value, cost, feasibility, dependencies and timing.

A flood barrier may protect stock and continuity. A redesigned distribution network may also support entry into a new market. Assess each benefit separately, including the investment required and the evidence behind expected demand.
