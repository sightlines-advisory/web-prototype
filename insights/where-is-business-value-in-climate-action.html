Where is the business value in climate action?

Company climate action is increasing and becoming more integrated in company strategy. Business value from this action is less visible. New international research highlights the difference between having initiatives underway and linking them to commercial success.

Explore the foundations: How do climate risks affect company value?

Climate transition is changing the conditions in which businesses compete, invest and grow.

Customer requirements are evolving. Technologies are becoming more competitive. Energy, insurance and financing conditions are changing. Physical climate impacts are affecting the reliability and cost of business operations.

These changes create risks, but also opportunities to improve productivity, strengthen customer relationships, develop new products and services, and increase financial flexibility.

For CEOs and Managing Directors, the challenge is to increase climate activity that strengthens the enterprise, by establishing the case for investment, and organising the capabilities to deliver.

The question for leaders is defining the case for a portfolio of climate action that will create and protect enterprise value.

The value delivery gap

South Pole's research suggests that climate activity is considerably more common than the organisational arrangements their research associates with effective implementation. This suggests that a company may have projects underway without having integrated climate in strategy, tested the financial case for investment, or secured the resources needed to deliver.

The figures describe different aspects of respondents' experience. They do not establish how many businesses have realised financial value from their climate initiatives.

Strategy and investment approvals are distinct survey measures, not sequential stages of a single group.

Value creation changes the conversation

South Pole defines its Advanced group using four measurable criteria:

  1. An approved climate strategy under implementation.
  2. Value creation and value protection included in climate planning.
  3. Physical and transition climate risks quantified or financially integrated.
  4. A dedicated transition capital or operating budget.

South Pole separately identifies four broader implementation building blocks: deciding where to act, securing commitment, mobilising delivery and measuring results. These are not the same as the four criteria used to define the Advanced survey group, which included 71 of 600 respondents. The broader building blocks encompass 15 assessed criteria.

Respondents in the Advanced group were more likely to report operating efficiencies, new customers and entry into new markets.

These findings are associations, not proof of cause and effect. They do not establish that adopting the four criteria will produce the same results for an individual Australian company.

Their value is in directing attention to the conditions leaders can investigate: strategic clarity, financial analysis, investment discipline and delivery capability.

These are reported associations, rather than proof of cause and effect. They suggest questions worth investigating in the enterprise, rather than guaranteed outcomes.

The commercial value of climate action becomes clearer when we consider how leaders with different responsibilities assess the same opportunity.

ILLUSTRATIVE COMPANY

Verdant Grid Technologies

This fictional Australian business develops battery storage and energy management solutions for commercial customers. Its enterprise value depends on technology performance, customer adoption, successful delivery and the ability to finance growth. Changing energy markets and customer requirements could create opportunities. But demand for lower-emissions and more resilient energy systems does not automatically translate into profitable growth.

Maya Chen — Founder and Managing Director

Maya sees an opportunity to expand the business by solving customers' problems with energy costs, reliability and flexibility.

Leadership question: Which customer problems could we solve in ways that create a durable commercial advantage?

Daniel Okafor — Chief Financial Officer

Daniel wants to understand the capital required to develop and deliver solutions, the timing of customer revenue, and the financial flexibility available if growth takes longer than expected.

Leadership question: What commercial and financial information would justify committing capital, and what would need to be true for the investment to create enterprise value?

Maya and Daniel are considering the same opportunity through different responsibilities. One is looking for growth and competitive advantage. The other is testing investment returns, financing capacity and delivery risk. Their perspectives are complementary. A credible investment decision needs both.

Start with the business you have

Every organisation creates value differently. Some depend on efficient assets and reliable supply chains. Others depend on intellectual property, customer relationships, specialist skills, trusted brands or access to finance.

Climate transition can change the performance of these drivers and create opportunities for new products, services, capabilities and partnerships. The starting point is to understand what matters most to the business, then test where investment could make it stronger.

What makes a climate opportunity worth investing in?

Climate-related opportunities should be examined with the same discipline as other enterprise investments.

Five questions help leaders establish the case for actions that address climate risks and opportunities.

  • Value proposition: What customer, operational or market problem will the investment solve?
  • Enterprise evidence: What do customers, operations, assets and commercial records tell us?
  • Financial consequences: How could revenue, margins, capital requirements and cash flows change?
  • Dependencies: Which technologies, suppliers, capabilities and partnerships must deliver?
  • Timing and flexibility: When must leaders act, and what choices could remain open if assumptions change?

An opportunity becomes an investment proposition when leaders can explain how value might be created, what it will cost to pursue, and which assumptions could change the outcome.

This does not require certainty. It requires sufficient information to compare alternatives and make a defensible decision.

From insight to investment

At Sightlines Advisory, the Climate Value Framework begins with the enterprise, not the climate reporting requirement.

It connects the sources of value already present in the business with climate-related risks and opportunities, enterprise evidence, plausible futures and financial consequences.

Five connected questions guide the investigation:

  1. What creates value in your business today?
  2. Which climate risks and opportunities affect those sources of value?
  3. What do your own business records and experience tell you?
  4. How might the financial impacts change under plausible future scenarios?
  5. Which responses create and protect the greatest value, and when?

This approach helps leaders recognise where climate-related changes could affect performance, compare possible responses and determine where, when and how to act.

Evidence developed for enterprise decisions can also provide a stronger foundation for climate-related financial disclosures.

The aim is to make climate considerations part of how the business creates, protects and allocates value—not a separate reporting exercise.

For your next leadership discussion, select one climate-related initiative already underway or under consideration.

Ask what enterprise value it is intended to create or protect, what commercial information supports that expectation, and which assumptions would cause you to reconsider the investment.

Climate action earns its place in business strategy when its contribution to performance can be investigated, compared and governed alongside other investments.
